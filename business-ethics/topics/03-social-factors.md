## Source: Professor's review lecture (~20:00 - 28:00), Canvas Weeks 9-10

## Why This Matters

Even people with good values and intentions can do unethical things because of social pressure. Understanding these mechanisms is how you PROTECT yourself from them.

---

## Asch Conformity Studies

### What Happened
Participants were asked to match line lengths. Confederate actors all gave wrong answers. How often did real participants go along with the obviously wrong answer?

### Key Findings
- People conform at surprisingly high rates - even with OBJECTIVELY wrong answers
- You are MORE likely to conform with STRANGERS than with people you know well
  - More troubling because you don't know strangers' moral history or commitments
- Conformity creates a brain-level "error signal" - it is physically uncomfortable to disagree with a group
  - Anticipate this feeling - it will happen when you're the odd one out
  - Knowing it's coming reduces its power

### The One Dissenting Voice Finding (EXAM-IMPORTANT)
Adding just ONE person who gives the correct answer DRAMATICALLY reduces conformity in everyone else.

Practical application: if something feels wrong in your organization, find ONE other person who agrees. That coalition of two is powerful.

Sharon Watkins at Enron: found Cliff Baxter. Together, they were able to blow the whistle where neither could have done it alone.

## Role-Binding (Stanford Prison Experiment - Zimbardo)

### What Happened
College students randomly assigned as guards or prisoners. No training. Stopped early because behavior became dangerous.

### Core Concept
When placed in a ROLE, your brain activates scripts and schemas for that role. These scripts can OVERRIDE:
- Your normal personality
- Your moral commitments and sensitivities

### Connection to Milgram
Zimbardo and Milgram were both trying to understand how ordinary people participated in WWII atrocities in Germany. Part of the answer: obedience to authority + role-binding.

### Implication for You
Ask yourself: What roles will I be placed in? How deeply have I internalized my moral identity so that no role can overwhelm it?

Personal code of ethics is your armor against role-binding.

### The Crucial Point
In EVERY iteration of these studies, there were always SOME people who refused to conform. We always have a choice. Believing that is one of the most powerful things a person can hold.

## Milgram Syndrome

### What Happened
Participants asked to administer electric shocks to actors who appeared to be in pain. Most continued when authority figure said "you have no choice."

### The Milgram Syndrome Components
1. Authority figure who gives instructions
2. Responsibility vacuum - "they told me to do it" / diffusion of moral responsibility
3. Incremental escalation - each step is only slightly worse than the last

### The Man Who Refused
One participant immediately responded: "I have a lot of choices. My worst choice is not hurting this man if I don't have to." Then walked out.

He had something - a deeply internalized moral identity - that others didn't. That's what personal code of ethics is meant to build.

## Moral Courage

### Definition (know all 3 conditions)
Moral courage is ONLY relevant when ALL THREE are present:

1. **Commitment to moral principles** - you must actually have principles to defend
2. **Risk / danger / sacrifice** - there must be something to lose (no risk = no need for courage)
3. **Willingness to endure** - moral courage usually unfolds over MONTHS or YEARS, not one moment

"We think of courage as doing something heroic in a punctuated quick moment, but often moral courage is needed across a long period of time." - Prof (verbatim)

Sharon Watkins, Worldcom whistleblowers - months of difficult endurance.

## Methods of Dissent

Two types - know the difference and when to use each:

### Direct Dissent
- Say clearly: "This is wrong" / "I won't do this"
- Use when: TIME is short, something is ABOUT to happen, harm is imminent
- Example: Challenger O-ring engineers - "We should NOT launch, the O-rings may freeze"
  - That was a moment for direct, unambiguous dissent

### Indirect Dissent (try first in most cases)
1. Ask for more information (slows things down, may reveal oversight)
2. Frame the issue around ORGANIZATIONAL VALUES ("This contradicts who we say we are as a company")
3. Creatively seek a third alternative that meets multiple needs
4. Build a coalition (ties back to the Asch finding)

"Framing things in terms of organizational values is a great strategy that doesn't put yourself on a moral pedestal - it elevates everyone."

---

## Obedience and Dissent Spectrum

The class covered a range from full obedience to full dissent:
- Full obedience: do exactly what you're told, no questions
- Indirect methods: work within the system, use soft approaches
- Direct dissent: clearly state disagreement
- Whistleblowing: go outside the organization/hierarchy

Most situations call for starting at indirect and moving toward direct only if necessary.

---

## How to Protect Yourself (Prof's Takeaways)

1. Know these mechanisms exist - awareness is armor
2. Build a deeply rooted moral identity (personal code of ethics)
3. Find at least one ally in any organization where you sense ethical problems
4. Anticipate the uncomfortable error signal when you dissent - it's normal, push through it
5. Remember: you ALWAYS have a choice
