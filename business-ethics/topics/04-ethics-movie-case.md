# Topic 4: Ethics Movie Case
## Source: Professor's review lecture (~24:00 - 32:00), Canvas Week 10

---

## Purpose of the Ethics Movie Case

Three different films, each teaching a distinct moral lesson through a real/dramatized case. The professor called this "a memorable way to paint three different types of moral scenarios that required moral courage or teach a unique moral lesson."

---

## Film 1: A Man for All Seasons (Thomas More)

### The Situation
Thomas More, as Lord Chancellor of England, refused to endorse King Henry VIII's break from the Catholic Church and his marriage to Anne Boleyn. He was progressively stripped of his position, wealth, and ultimately executed.

### Key Lessons

**Sustained moral courage under mounting pressure**
- Everything was taken from him incrementally
- He maintained his integrity the entire time
- His integrity was THAT valuable to him

**He used INDIRECT dissent**
- Never directly said "This is wrong" to the king
- Strategy: stayed silent, pleaded the fifth
- "I'm not going to say why I'm not supporting this, but I'm just not saying I support it"
- Silence used as a strategic tool, not cowardice

**Long arc of moral courage**
- This unfolded over years
- Moral courage is usually a marathon, not a sprint

## Film 2: 13 Lives (Dr. Harry Harris)

### The Situation
The Thai cave rescue. 12 boys and their soccer coach trapped in a flooding cave. International medical consent laws would normally prevent medical intervention without proper consent - but these rules were relaxed to save the boys.

### Key Lessons

**Weighing and prioritizing moral principles when rules don't apply**
- "In wartime decisions, in like the 13 lives when people are going to die and you should do something, you have to decide: there's typical rules, but this is a very unique atypical situation"
- No rule covers every situation - especially new technology-driven dilemmas
- You need moral PRINCIPLES to fall back on when rules don't exist

**What happens in courts of law without applicable rules?**
- Judges scrutinize: What were your motives? What were your intentions? Did you act in good faith?
- Even without laws, intentions and principles matter
- "Having moral principles to rely on when rules and laws and regulations are not there yet is really important"

**Application: New technology**
- Self-driving cars, AI, biotech - new situations create new dilemmas with no rules yet
- Moral principles carry you where rules haven't been written

## Film 3: Quiz Show (Charles Van Doren)

### The Situation
The 1950s TV game show "Twenty-One" was rigged. Charles Van Doren, a well-educated idealist from a famous family, was given answers in advance to help him win. The fraud was eventually exposed.

### Key Lessons

**Repeated exposure to temptation wears down resolve**
- Van Doren initially said no, this seems wrong
- But "the opportunity kept coming and kept coming"
- Then rationalization set in (La Fontaine: being "poisoned by degrees")
- Application: One-time temptations are easier to resist than sustained, incremental ones

**Rationalization dressed as idealism**
- Producers told Van Doren: "You will inspire education in America"
- They spoke HIS language - appealing to his idealism
- But it was moral rationalization, not genuine moral principle
- "Time can weaken resolve when you're surrounded by people with contrary ethical standards"

**The weak actor takes the fall**
- Van Doren - the participant, not the organizer - suffered the most reputational damage
- Executives got "slap on the wrist," returned to industry in 2 years making millions
- Van Doren never recovered reputationally
- Lesson: in organizational scandals, those with less power often pay the highest price

## The Self-Growth Model (Underlying All Three)

The professor referenced the Self-Growth Model as the framework that connects all three films.

The idea: deliberate character building is the DEFENSE against these situations.

"You need something more than intelligence and a general desire to do what's right. There are situations that will overwhelm that."

What you need:
- Deeply driven moral commitments (not just casual good intentions)
- Something more than just wanting to do right
- Example: Richard Wilson ("I would rather go bankrupt than not honor my commitment")
