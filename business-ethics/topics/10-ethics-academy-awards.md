## Source: Professor's review lecture (~66:00 - end), Canvas Week 14-15

## What This Is About

Preparing for HIGH-STAKES CONVERSATIONS where:
- Emotions are elevated
- Disagreement is likely
- Moral principles are colliding
- The outcome is pivotal for your career or organization

"You can work hard and have good performance, but then some of these conversations go extremely poorly and it can reduce a lot of that positive favor."

---

## Moral Muteness vs Moral Superiority

**Moral muteness** = staying silent about an ethical issue to avoid conflict
- The problem: the issue doesn't go away, you lose the chance to influence it
- Often happens when people feel outranked or fear social consequences

**Moral superiority** = acting as if you are more ethical than everyone else
- The problem: creates defensiveness, destroys relationship capital
- Makes you a crusader/martyr rather than a diplomat

Both are failure modes. The goal is ethical advocacy without either silence or self-righteousness.

## Preparing for the Crucial Conversation (from GVV + Ethics Academy Awards)

### Step 1: Identify which moral principles are in conflict
- Name them explicitly
- Example: "This situation pits employee loyalty against honesty to customers"
- You can't solve it if you can't name it

### Step 2: Look for the creative third alternative
- Most dilemmas are presented as binary: A or B
- "In the dilemma there's usually a binary set of responses"
- The skilled ethical leader finds option C that satisfies multiple principles
- Example: instead of "fire the employee" or "cover up the mistake" -> "remediate with customer + develop the employee"

### Step 3: Frame around organizational values (not personal morality)
- Don't: "I personally think this is wrong"
- Do: "This seems to conflict with the culture we've been trying to build here"
- This elevates everyone rather than putting you on a moral pedestal
- Reduces defensiveness

### Step 4: Use your voice strengths
- Back to Giving Voice to Values (Topic 5)
- Know whether you lead with stories, logic, coalitions, or pragmatism

## Responding to Unethical Requests from Power

When a boss or authority figure asks you to do something unethical:

**Suggested sequence** (start indirect, go direct only if needed):

1. Ask for more information
   - "Help me understand the intent here..."
   - Buys time, may reveal they haven't thought it through
   - Sometimes the request dissolves when they explain it

2. Express concern through org values
   - "I want to make sure this aligns with our commitment to X"
   - Positions you as a team player, not a rebel

3. Seek creative alternatives
   - "What if we approached it this way instead?"
   - Shows you're trying to solve the problem, not just block it

4. Direct refusal (last resort)
   - "I'm not able to do this"
   - Use when there is no alternative and harm is certain
   - Keep it professional, not personal

## Preparing in Advance

The professor emphasized ADVANCE PREPARATION repeatedly.

Think through:
- What kinds of high-stakes conversations am I likely to face in my career/industry?
- What moral principles are typically in tension in those scenarios?
- What are my voice strengths?
- What are some stories or analogies I can use?
- What creative third alternatives might exist?

"I want to really help you get prepared to run into some of these high-stakes crucial conversations."
