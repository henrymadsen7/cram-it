# Topic 5: Giving Voice to Values / Magic Kingdom Case
## Source: Professor's review lecture (~28:00 - 40:00), Canvas Week 12

---

## The Core Idea (Mary Gentile's Work)

Most ethics education teaches you to RECOGNIZE ethical issues and REASON about them.

Giving Voice to Values (GVV) asks a different question: assuming you know what the right thing is, how do you actually SAY IT and make it happen?

"Preparation will be miles ahead of others in advocating an ethical position when you embrace what Gentile is talking about."

This is practical, not theoretical.

---

## The Magic Kingdom Case Context

In class, you debated a fictional theme park scenario with secret instructions. You were already making values propositions - advocating a position based on values, not just rules.

Key insight: values propositions are harder when:
- There is no concrete rule or law to point to
- The other person doesn't share your value

Most real ethical situations in business fall into this category. Rules don't cover everything.

---

## Step 1: Know Your Voice Strengths

Everyone has natural strengths in how they communicate and persuade. Know yours.

4 types:
1. **Metaphors and Stories** - you communicate best through narrative, analogies, examples
2. **Building Coalitions** - you persuade by gathering allies and showing shared concern
3. **Pragmatism** - you lead with practical consequences, risks, efficiency arguments
4. **Logic and Reason** - you persuade through systematic argument and evidence

Why this matters: play to your strengths. If you're not a confrontational person, build a coalition instead of demanding direct confrontation. If you're a storyteller, lead with a story, not a spreadsheet.

---

## Step 2: Anticipate Common Dilemma Structures

You won't know the exact details of future ethical dilemmas. But you CAN know their general structure.

Common types of tensions:
- **Justice vs Mercy** - what is fair vs what is compassionate?
- **Utilitarianism vs Deontology** - greatest good for greatest number vs right by duty regardless of outcome
- **Long term vs Short term** - what helps now vs what serves us better later?
- **Honesty vs Loyalty** - telling the truth vs protecting someone you care about

For each type, prepare:
- A story or metaphor that makes your point
- A practical argument
- A values-framing statement

"Even though you won't know the specifics and nuance of the dilemma you may be in the future, you can anticipate the general structure."

---

## Step 3: Adopt the Right Posture

Better posture = **diplomat / team player**
Worse posture = crusader or martyr

Crusaders and martyrs:
- Make it about them being morally right
- Create confrontation and defensiveness
- Often lose influence even when they're right

Diplomats and team players:
- Frame the issue around shared organizational values
- Look for third alternatives that satisfy multiple principles
- Keep relationships intact
- Often more effective at actually changing behavior

---

## Values Propositions vs Rule Propositions

Rule proposition: "This violates policy 4.2.1, we can't do this"
Values proposition: "I think this conflicts with our commitment to honesty with customers"

Values propositions:
- Are necessary when there is no applicable rule
- Are harder because the other person may not share the value
- Require more preparation and skillfulness
- Are what you will actually face most often as a manager

---

## Practical Prep Strategy

For each of the 4 common dilemma types, have ready:
1. One story or metaphor
2. One practical/pragmatic argument
3. A way to frame it around organizational values

Example for Justice vs Mercy:
- Story: "Think about when your professor gave you a second chance even though you missed the deadline. What did that do for your loyalty and effort in the class?"
- Practical: "If we show mercy here, we invest in this relationship and they will work harder for us long-term"
- Org values: "We say we believe in giving people a chance to grow - that's the culture we're trying to build"
