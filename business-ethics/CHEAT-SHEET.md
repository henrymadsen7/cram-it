## 1. LEADER HUMILITY (HIGH)

3 dimensions in developmental sequence:
  1. Self-Awareness (illumination stage) - know your strengths AND limits
  2. Teachability - being grounded = open to wisdom/feedback
  3. Appreciation of Others / Seeking Mentors (micro-mentors)

Accurate sense of self = measured on INTEGRITY + ABILITY
  - Inflated sense = overclaims, rejects feedback (Enron's Andy Fastow, swollen ankle analogy)
  - Compressed sense = underperforms, fails to act (also not humility, also a problem)

Tactics:
  - TAGS list (Talents, Achievements, Goals, Service) = decompression tool
  - SWOT analysis = deflation tool for the inflated

Key case: Steve Jobs 1.0 (toxic) vs 2.0 (tempered narcissist) - humility made Apple #1

Titanic (arrogance) vs Olympic (humility mindset)

Humility = temperance virtue, keeps you in the Aristotelian golden mean

## 2. COMMODIFICATION (HIGH)

Market economy vs market society - problem is when market logic invades everything

5 tests for whether a good/service SHOULD be commoditized:
  1. Fairness / equality - does it unfairly impact anyone?
  2. Coercion / rights violations
  3. Corruption - does it treat something with a lower mode of valuation than appropriate?
  4. Social value - does it create or destroy social value?
  5. Proper mode of valuation - is money the right way to value this?

Cases:
  - Disneyland disabled-person line skipping = raises corruption/dignity questions
  - Project Prevention (Barbara Harris) = paying drug-addicted mothers for sterilization = corruption test
  - Police car ads = impartiality / dignity of office concerns
  - Pinto + dark side of goal setting = Ford charged with homicide, overworked recall coordinator
  - Wells Fargo = perverse incentives from quotas -> fraud (signed up customers without consent)

## 3. SOCIAL FACTORS & MORAL COURAGE (HIGH)

Asch Conformity Studies:
  - People conform even when objectively wrong
  - MORE likely to conform with STRANGERS than with people you know
  - One dissenting voice drastically reduces conformity - find your ally
  - Neurological: disagreeing with the group creates a painful error signal

Role-Binding (Stanford Prison Experiment - Zimbardo):
  - Roles activate scripts that can override your moral identity
  - Even without training, students acted out of character
  - Solution: drive moral identity so deep no role can overwhelm it

Milgram Syndrome:
  - Obedience to authority can cause people to harm others
  - Components: authority figure, responsibility vacuum, incremental escalation
  - The man who said "I have a lot of choices" - always remember you have a choice

Moral Courage = requires all 3:
  1. Commitment to moral principles
  2. Risk / danger / sacrifice present
  3. Willingness to endure (usually a long process, not a single moment)

Methods of Dissent:
  - Direct dissent = state clearly this is wrong (use when time-critical, e.g. Challenger O-rings)
  - Indirect dissent (try first): ask for more information, frame around org values, seek third alternatives

Sharon Watkins at Enron: found one ally (Cliff Baxter), together blew the whistle

## 4. ETHICS MOVIE CASE (MEDIUM)

3 films, 3 lessons:
  1. A Man for All Seasons (Thomas More) = sustained moral courage under mounting pressure
     - Used INDIRECT dissent - never said "this is wrong" to the king, just stayed silent
     - Everything was taken from him but his integrity
  2. 13 Lives = weighing competing principles when normal rules don't apply
     - Medical consent laws relaxed to save the boys
     - Wartime-level decisions: what moral principles remain when rules are gone?
  3. Quiz Show (Charles Van Doren) = repeated exposure to temptation wears down resolve
     - Rationalization dressed as idealism (producers said "you'll inspire education")
     - Weak actor (Van Doren) took the fall while executives got slaps on wrist

Self-Growth Model: deliberate character building is the defense against these situations

## 5. GIVING VOICE TO VALUES - MAGIC KINGDOM (HIGH)

Core idea: you CAN anticipate ethical dilemmas and PREPARE in advance

4 Voice Strengths (know yours):
  1. Metaphors and stories
  2. Building coalitions
  3. Pragmatism / practical arguments
  4. Logic and reason

Common dilemma STRUCTURES (anticipate these):
  - Justice vs mercy
  - Utilitarianism vs deontology
  - Long term vs short term
  - Honesty vs loyalty

Better posture: diplomat / team player - NOT crusader or martyr

Values propositions: you are advocating a value (not a rule or law)
  - Harder when the other person doesn't share your value
  - Preparation makes you miles ahead of others

## 7. STAKEHOLDER THEORY (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

Primary stakeholders = formal contracts with company (employees, shareholders, customers)
Secondary stakeholders = no contract, but affected by company actions (community, environment)

Original theory fell apart = "too many stakeholders, can't manage all of them"
Milton Friedman counter-argument: just maximize shareholder value

SALIENCE MODEL (Mitchell, Agle, Wood) = the solution - KNOW THIS COLD:

3 dimensions:
  - POWER - can they affect the company?
  - LEGITIMACY - do they have a moral/ethical claim on the company?
  - URGENCY - is their claim time-sensitive?

Venn Diagram categories (overlaps of P, L, U):
  - All 3 (P+L+U) = DEFINITIVE stakeholder - top priority, must address
  - P+L only = DOMINANT stakeholder
  - P+U only = DANGEROUS stakeholder (watch out)
  - L+U only = DEPENDENT stakeholder (needs advocacy)
  - Only 1 attribute = LATENT stakeholders (low priority)
  - None = NON-STAKEHOLDER (not your concern)

Key distinction: the model tells you WHO IS IN and WHO IS OUT

Use case: Tesla self-driving cars - who do you invite? Use salience to prioritize

Pros of stakeholder theory: long-term thinking, CSR, acknowledges externalities
Cons: less nimble decisions, pressure to waste money on performative CSR

## 8. CONFLICT OF INTEREST (HIGH)

Default rule = ALWAYS DISCLOSE

When you have a conflict: your role may compromise your objectivity
  - You are hired to make decisions in the org's best interest
  - Another role (family member, investor, friend) may pull that objectivity

Steps:
  1. Disclose to relevant parties
  2. Withdraw if necessary (step back from the decision)

Prof's example: reviewing a paper from scholars on his own Templeton grant = withdrew

Nepotism = classic conflict of interest (family role vs organizational role)

## 9. DIVERSITY & AFFIRMATIVE ACTION (HIGH)

Why diversity matters: adds creativity, specialization, nuanced perspectives

Diversity vs Affirmative Action - KNOW THE DIFFERENCE:
  - Diversity initiative = voluntary, broad, ongoing effort to recruit diverse candidates
  - Affirmative action = legally specific program, must meet legal requirements

LEGAL STATUS - this may be a true/false question:
  - Affirmative action in EDUCATION = banned (Supreme Court ruling vs Harvard, U of Michigan)
    - You CANNOT use race as a specific numeric factor
    - You CAN consider race if applicant explains how their background adds value (case-by-case)
  - Affirmative action in EMPLOYMENT = still legal in most states
    - Banned in California, Michigan, Washington
    - Where legal: must be WRITTEN DOWN, must prove historic disparity, must end when disparity is rectified

Human capital decisions = legally riskiest area → always consult HR/legal

## 10. ETHICS ACADEMY AWARDS / CRUCIAL CONVERSATIONS (MEDIUM)

Moral muteness = staying silent about an ethical issue to avoid conflict (problem)
Moral superiority = acting as if you are more ethical than others (also a problem)

Preparing for high-stakes conversations:
  1. Identify WHICH moral principles are in conflict
  2. Look for a CREATIVE THIRD ALTERNATIVE that satisfies multiple principles
  3. Play to your voice strengths
  4. Frame around organizational values (not personal morality)

Responding to unethical requests from someone in power:
  - Ask for more info first (buy time, may reveal the person didn't think it through)
  - Frame around shared org values
  - Seek alternatives
  - Direct refusal as last resort when stakes are high

## 11. EARLY WEEKS - FOUNDATIONS (MEDIUM)

Why be ethical?
  - False dichotomy: "I can be successful OR ethical"
  - Ethics reduces transaction costs (trust = efficiency)
  - Psychological/physiological benefits

Ethical Reasoning Frameworks (know all 4):
  - Virtue Ethics = what kind of person does this make me?
  - Utilitarianism = greatest good for greatest number
  - Deontology = rule-based, duty-bound regardless of outcome
  - Social Contract = what would a reasonable society agree to?

Kohlberg's Moral Development (3 levels, 6 stages):
  - Pre-Conventional = self-interest (avoid punishment, seek reward)
  - Conventional = rules, social norms, loyalty
  - Post-Conventional = principles, universal ethics, social contract

Moral Regulation:
  - Moral licensing = past good behavior gives you psychological "permission" to do wrong
  - Moral cleansing = doing good to atone for past wrong
  - Prescriptive ethics = do good proactively
  - Proscriptive ethics = avoid doing wrong

Moral Rationalization tactics (know the types):
  - Denial of responsibility
  - Denial of injury
  - Appeal to higher loyalties
  - Condemnation of condemners
  - Displacement of responsibility

Self-awareness tools: revisionary ethics, forecasting errors, Self-Growth Model

CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility):
  - Purpose: shoulder externalities, serve all stakeholders
  - Caution: can backfire if performative / wasteful
  - Prof's view: a genuine CSR commitment has a strong business case
