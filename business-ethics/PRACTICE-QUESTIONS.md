## SECTION 1: Leader Humility

**Q1.** What are the three dimensions of leader humility in developmental sequence?

> Self-awareness (illumination) -> Teachability -> Appreciation of others / seeking mentors. They spiral upward and build on each other.

**Q2.** What is the difference between an inflated and compressed sense of self?

> Inflated = exaggerated positive perception of your ability and integrity. Overclaims, rejects feedback, defensive. (Example: Andy Fastow)
> Compressed = underestimates own strengths. Fails to act, fails to use gifts for good. Neither is humility - both are self-deception.

**Q3.** Steve Jobs was a "tempered narcissist." What does this mean?

> Jobs 1.0 was caustic and toxic - liquidating value. After being fired from Apple, he embraced humility. Jobs 2.0 still had his ambition and vision but acknowledged mistakes, credited others, and built team trust. His narcissism was tempered but not eliminated.

**Q4.** What is the TAGS list and what problem does it solve?

> Talents, Achievements, Goals, Service. It is a decompression tool for a COMPRESSED sense of self. Helps you build a realistic view of your strengths so you can acknowledge limitations without threatening your sense of self.

**Q5.** What is humility as a temperance virtue?

> It keeps other virtues in the Aristotelian "golden mean." Courage without humility becomes recklessness. Confidence without humility becomes arrogance. Humility calibrates.

## SECTION 2: Commodification

**Q6.** What are the 5 tests for whether a good or service should be commoditized?

> 1. Fairness / equality (does it create unfair advantage?)
> 2. Coercion / rights violations
> 3. Corruption (treating with lower mode of valuation than appropriate)
> 4. Social value (creates or destroys?)
> 5. Proper mode of valuation (is money the right way to value this?)

**Q7.** What was the main ethical problem with Project Prevention?

> Barbara Harris' organization paid drug-addicted mothers for sterilization. Main ethical issue = the CORRUPTION test: treating the capacity to have children as a commodity, offering to "turn it off for a fee." Also raises coercion concerns (offering money to people in desperate situations).

**Q8.** What was the "dark side of goal setting" in the Pinto and Wells Fargo cases?

> Pinto: Ford set quotas without adequate staff, leading to a recall coordinator being overworked and missing safety issues. Ford became the first company charged with homicide.
> Wells Fargo: Corporate sign-up quotas were so aggressive that local branches created fraudulent accounts to avoid being fired. Classic perverse incentive from goal pressure.

## SECTION 3: Social Factors

**Q9.** What did Asch's conformity studies show?

> People conform with objectively wrong answers under social pressure. More likely to conform with strangers than people you know. It creates a neurological error signal (it feels uncomfortable to be the odd one out). One dissenting voice dramatically reduces everyone's conformity.

**Q10.** What is role-binding? Give an example.

> When placed in a specific role, the brain activates scripts and schemas for that role. These can override normal personality and moral commitments. Example: Stanford Prison Experiment - college students randomly assigned as guards became cruel, even with no training and knowing it was a study.

**Q11.** What are the three requirements for moral courage?

> 1. Commitment to moral principles
> 2. Risk / danger / sacrifice present
> 3. Willingness to endure (usually a long process, not a single moment)

**Q12.** Your boss is pressuring you to falsify a report. Using methods of dissent, what do you do first?

> Try INDIRECT methods first: ask for more information, frame the concern around organizational values, seek a creative alternative. Only move to DIRECT refusal if time is short or indirect methods fail. Direct: "I'm not able to do this because it conflicts with our commitment to accurate reporting."

## SECTION 4: Ethics Movies

**Q13.** What three ethical lessons did the three films teach?

> A Man for All Seasons (Thomas More): Sustained moral courage under mounting pressure; using indirect dissent (silence) strategically; integrity is worth more than comfort.
> 13 Lives: Weighing and prioritizing moral principles when rules don't apply; having principles to rely on when laws haven't been written yet.
> Quiz Show (Van Doren): Repeated exposure to temptation erodes resolve; rationalization dressed as idealism; weak actors often take the fall while powerful executives escape.

**Q14.** How did Thomas More use dissent without ever directly confronting the king?

> He stayed silent and "pleaded the fifth" - never said "this is wrong" directly. He declined to say why he wasn't supporting the king, but refused to declare his support. Strategic indirect dissent.

## SECTION 5: Giving Voice to Values

**Q15.** What are the 4 voice strengths in Giving Voice to Values?

> 1. Metaphors and stories
> 2. Building coalitions
> 3. Pragmatism / practical arguments
> 4. Logic and reason

**Q16.** What are the 4 common dilemma structures you should anticipate?

> 1. Justice vs mercy
> 2. Utilitarianism vs deontology
> 3. Long term vs short term
> 4. Honesty vs loyalty

**Q17.** Why is being a "diplomat/team player" better than being a "crusader" when advocating ethically?

> Crusaders make it about personal moral superiority, create defensiveness, and often lose influence even when right. Diplomats frame issues around shared organizational values, seek third alternatives, keep relationships intact, and are MORE effective at actually changing behavior.

## SECTION 6: BYU Grads in the Workplace

**Q18.** Why is building relational capital important for ethical leadership?

> Without trust and relationships, your ethical concerns will be ignored or dismissed. You need relational capital BEFORE you need to use it. Build it by being professional, showing up, being genuinely interested in coworkers - then when you need to advocate ethically, people listen.

**Q19.** What is the "Fairness for All" principle?

> Protecting BOTH religious liberty AND LGBTQ+ rights simultaneously. Not a zero-sum conflict. Utah/Salt Lake were praised for innovative legislation that protected both classes at once. The principle: find the overlapping space where multiple groups are protected.

## SECTION 7: STAKEHOLDER THEORY (HIGHEST)

**Q20.** Draw and label the Stakeholder Salience Venn diagram from memory.

> Three circles: P (Power), L (Legitimacy), U (Urgency)
> P+L+U = Definitive (top priority)
> P+L = Dominant
> L+U = Dependent
> P+U = Dangerous
> Single attribute = Dormant / Discretionary / Demanding (latent)
> None = Non-stakeholder

**Q21.** What is the difference between power, legitimacy, and urgency in the salience model?

> Power: Can they affect the company? Do they have leverage?
> Legitimacy: Do they have a moral/ethical claim on the company? Is there a commonly agreed moral connection between company actions and their interests?
> Urgency: Is their claim time-sensitive or critical?

**Q22.** Why did the original stakeholder theory fall out of favor?

> Companies said it was too unwieldy - too many stakeholders to manage. They defaulted to "just maximize shareholder value." The salience model solved this by giving a practical framework for deciding WHO IS IN vs WHO IS OUT.

**Q23.** Tesla is developing a self-driving car decision system. Who are the stakeholders? Categorize three of them.

> Passengers (Power to buy/not buy + Legitimacy as users affected by safety + Urgency if accident risk is immediate) = Definitive
> Insurance companies (Power as major payers + Legitimacy over risk/liability) = Dominant
> Bystanders/pedestrians (Legitimacy - direct moral claim as people who could be hit + Urgency if accidents happen) = Dependent (need advocacy since they have no power over Tesla)
> Future legislation writers (Power eventually + Legitimacy) = currently may be Dormant

**Q24.** What are the pros and cons of stakeholder theory?

> Pros: Long-term strategic thinking, promotes CSR, acknowledges externalities, avoids blindside lawsuits
> Cons: Slows decision-making, can pressure wasteful/performative CSR, Friedman argument that it spends shareholders' money without authorization

## SECTION 8: Conflict of Interest

**Q25.** What is the default rule when you perceive a conflict of interest?

> DISCLOSE. Always. Then withdraw if necessary.

**Q26.** Your uncle's company is one of three vendors being evaluated for a contract at your company. What do you do?

> Immediately disclose to your supervisor that you have a family relationship with one of the vendors. Then either: withdraw from the evaluation process entirely, or if you must be involved, ensure the conflict is transparent to all decision-makers. Don't try to "be objective anyway" - the appearance of conflict matters.

## SECTION 9: Diversity & Affirmative Action

**Q27.** True or False: Affirmative action is unconstitutional.

> FALSE. It is heavily constrained but not unconstitutional. In education: race-conscious admissions is banned. In employment: still legal in most states, but must be written, prove historical disparity, and end when disparity is rectified.

**Q28.** What can a university legally do regarding race in admissions?

> They CANNOT use race as a numerical factor or quota system. They CAN consider race if an applicant explains in their essay how their racial/ethnic background and related experiences (overcoming discrimination, unique perspective) would add sociological value to the university community - case by case only.

## SECTION 10: Crucial Conversations

**Q29.** What is the difference between moral muteness and moral superiority?

> Moral muteness: staying silent about an ethical issue to avoid conflict. Problem: the issue doesn't get addressed.
> Moral superiority: acting as if you are more ethical than others, putting yourself on a moral pedestal. Problem: creates defensiveness and destroys influence.
> Both are failure modes.

**Q30.** What is a "creative third alternative"?

> In most ethical dilemmas you're presented with a binary: A or B. A creative third alternative (option C) satisfies multiple moral principles simultaneously, rather than sacrificing one for the other. Example: instead of "cover up the mistake" or "fire the person" -> "remediate with customers AND develop the employee."

## SECTION 11: Foundations

**Q31.** List the 4 ethical frameworks and their core question.

> Virtue ethics: What kind of person does this make me?
> Utilitarianism: What produces the greatest good for the greatest number?
> Deontology: What is my duty / what rule applies regardless of outcome?
> Social contract: What rules would rational people agree to for living together?

**Q32.** What is hyperbolic discounting and why is it ethically relevant?

> The tendency to overvalue immediate rewards vs future consequences. Makes short-term temptations feel more powerful than long-term consequences. This is why even smart, good people make bad ethical choices "just this once." Awareness of this bias is protective.

**Q33.** What is moral licensing? Give an example.

> Past good behavior creates psychological "permission" to slip. Example: "I've been honest all week, it's fine to exaggerate this one figure on the report." The problem: ethical behavior is not a bank account where good deeds create credit for future bad ones.

- What should she do? (Indirect dissent first: ask for more info, frame around company values, seek alternative. Direct refusal if needed. Consider conflict of interest between bonus and org duty - disclose this to self/supervisor.)
- What are her voice strengths? (Depends on her profile - but she should prepare in advance using GVV framework)
